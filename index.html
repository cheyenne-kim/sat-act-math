What Actually Improves Math Scores

Most math mistakes are not knowledge gaps — they're execution gaps. Students know the formula but mis-apply it under time pressure. The fix is building clean, repeatable problem-solving habits.

1. Read the question twice.

Most wrong answers come from answering a different question than was asked.

2. Write every step.

Mental math causes arithmetic errors. Pencil-and-paper steps catch them.

3. Check your answer against the question.

Did you find x, or did the question ask for 2x + 1?

4. Know when to skip and return.

A hard problem at the end of an ACT section costs you easy points at the beginning of the next one.

Self-Diagnosis

Where You're Losing Points

Arithmetic errors on easy questions

Write out your steps. Don't do 3-step calculations in your head.

Misread word problems

Underline what the question is actually asking for before solving.

Forgot a formula

Memorize the 20 key formulas. The Formula Sheets tab has them all.

Ran out of time

Skip problems that will take more than 90 seconds. Come back with fresh eyes.

SAT-Specific Tips

Use Desmos aggressively

The built-in Desmos calculator can graph equations and find intersections. Use it for any problem involving a curve or system — it saves time on hard problems.

Read the reference sheet

The SAT provides a reference sheet with geometry formulas. Know what's on it so you don't waste time memorizing things you can look up — and know what's NOT on it (trig identities, quadratic formula).

Grid-in strategy

Double-check grid-ins — no answer choices to catch errors. Answers are never negative. If you get a fraction, simplify it before entering. Both fraction and decimal forms work.

Core Techniques

Five Math Strategies That Actually Work

1. Plug In Numbers

When a problem has variables in the answer choices, pick a simple number for the variable, solve with that number, then see which answer matches. Works great for percent, ratio, and "in terms of" questions.

Best for: "If x is increased by 20%, the new value is..." → Let x = 100, new value = 120, check choices.

2. Backsolve

When the answer choices are numbers, start with choice C (or B for ACT), substitute it back into the problem, and see if it works. If the result is too big, try a smaller choice; too small, try larger.

Best for: "If 3x + 5 = 20, what is x?" → Try C first, adjust direction.

3. Draw It Out

Geometry problems without diagrams should always get one. Label every angle and length the problem gives you. Often the answer becomes obvious once the diagram is complete.

Best for: Any geometry problem — especially word problems that describe a shape without showing it.

4. Estimate and Eliminate

For multi-step calculation problems, estimate the answer range first. Then eliminate answer choices that are clearly too big or small. Often you can narrow to one or two choices without full calculation.

Best for: Problems with widely spread answer choices (e.g., 5, 50, 500, 5000).

5. Watch the Question

The single most common math error is solving for x when the question asks for 2x, or finding the area when it asks for the perimeter. Underline what the question is asking for before you start calculating.

Watch for: "What is the value of x + 3?" after finding x = 5. The answer is 8, not 5.

Calculator Strategy

Use the calculator for arithmetic, not thinking. Set it up to check answers quickly. On the SAT, use Desmos to graph equations and find intersections visually — it's faster than solving algebraically for many problems.

Don't rely on it for: Order of operations, negative exponents, or substituting into expressions — do those steps by hand first.

Common Traps

How Test Makers Catch You

Partial answers in the choices

If the question asks for x + y, one of the wrong answers will be x alone and one will be y alone. Solve all the way.

Units confusion

Hours vs. minutes, feet vs. inches, miles vs. kilometers. Check that your units match before computing.

Exponent errors with negatives

(−3)² = 9 but −3² = −9. Parentheses matter. Write them out carefully.

Diagram not to scale (ACT)

ACT geometry diagrams may not be drawn to scale. Don't estimate angles visually — use the properties.

Forgetting to simplify

An unsimplified answer may match none of the choices. Always simplify fractions and radicals.

Time Management

Pacing Rules

ACT: 1 minute average

Bank time on Q1–20. Spend extra time on Q41–60. If a problem takes more than 90 seconds, skip and return.

SAT: ~95 seconds average

The adaptive format means hard problems are worth the same as easy ones. Don't over-invest in any single problem.

The 3-pass system

Pass 1: Answer every problem you can solve quickly. Pass 2: Return to medium-difficulty skips. Pass 3: Guess remaining with best elimination.

Never leave blank on ACT

No penalty for wrong answers. A random guess has a 20% chance of being right. A blank is always 0%.

Hard Question Types

Advanced Topics Deep Dive

These six topic areas generate the hardest questions on both the ACT and SAT. Mastering them is what separates a 30+ (ACT) or 700+ (SAT Math) from an average score.

Topic 1

Functions & Transformations

Function problems on hard questions go beyond simple substitution. You need to handle composition, inverses, piecewise definitions, and graph shifts.

Composition f(g(x))

Substitute g(x) wherever you see x in f. Work inside-out: evaluate g first, then plug result into f.

Inverse Functions f⁻¹(x)

Swap x and y, then solve for y. Verify: f(f⁻¹(x)) = x. The graph of f⁻¹ is a reflection of f over y = x.

Piecewise Functions

Determine which piece applies based on the input value. Don't mix up the domain conditions.

Graph Transformations

f(x − h) + k shifts right h and up k. f(−x) reflects over y-axis. −f(x) reflects over x-axis. f(2x) compresses horizontally by ½.

Worked Example

If f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x², find f(g(3)).

Step 1: g(3) = 3² = 9
Step 2: f(9) = 2(9) + 1 = 19

Worked Example — Inverse

Find the inverse of f(x) = 3x − 6.

Step 1: y = 3x − 6
Step 2: Swap: x = 3y − 6
Step 3: Solve: y = (x + 6)/3, so f⁻¹(x) = (x + 6)/3

Common Trap

f(g(x)) ≠ g(f(x)). The order matters — always check which function goes inside which.

Topic 2

Quadratics & Polynomials

Advanced quadratic questions go beyond basic factoring. Expect completing the square, Vieta's formulas, and the polynomial remainder theorem.

Completing the Square

x² + bx = (x + b/2)² − (b/2)². Use to find vertex form y = a(x − h)² + k or the center of a circle.

Vieta's Formulas

For ax² + bx + c = 0: sum of roots = −b/a, product of roots = c/a. Lets you find root sums/products without solving.

Polynomial Remainder Theorem

When polynomial p(x) is divided by (x − k), the remainder equals p(k). If p(k) = 0, then (x − k) is a factor.

Discriminant b² − 4ac

> 0: two real roots. = 0: one repeated root. < 0: no real roots (complex). Used to determine number of solutions.

Worked Example — Vieta's

For 2x² − 8x + 3 = 0, find the sum and product of the roots.

Sum: −b/a = −(−8)/2 = 4
Product: c/a = 3/2 = 1.5

Worked Example — Remainder Theorem

What is the remainder when x³ − 2x + 5 is divided by (x − 3)?

Plug in x = 3: (3)³ − 2(3) + 5 = 27 − 6 + 5 = 26

Common Trap

Vieta's sum = −b/a, NOT just −b. Always divide by the leading coefficient a.

Topic 3

Exponentials & Logarithms

The SAT heavily tests exponential models. The ACT favors solving exponential equations by matching bases. Both test log properties.

Growth / Decay Model

A = A₀ · bᵗ — identify the initial value A₀, base b, and time t. Growth: b > 1. Decay: 0 < b < 1.

Log Definition

log_b(x) = y means b^y = x. So log₂(8) = 3 because 2³ = 8.

Log Properties

log(ab) = log a + log b · log(a/b) = log a − log b · log(aⁿ) = n·log a

Solving Exponential Equations

Rewrite both sides with the same base, then set exponents equal. Or take log of both sides.

Worked Example — Decay Model

A car worth $20,000 loses 15% of its value each year. What is it worth after 3 years?

Model: A = 20000 · (0.85)³
= 20000 × 0.614125 ≈ $12,283

Worked Example — Logs

Solve: log₃(x) = 4

Convert: 3⁴ = x → x = 81

Common Trap

log(a + b) ≠ log a + log b. Log properties only apply to multiplication/division inside the log.

Topic 4

Statistics & Counting

Both tests hit combinatorics and probability. The SAT also tests conditional probability and data interpretation from two-way tables.

Permutations P(n, r)

Ordered arrangements of r items from n: P(n,r) = n! / (n−r)!. Use when order matters (e.g., race finishing positions).

Combinations C(n, r)

Unordered selections: C(n,r) = n! / [r!(n−r)!]. Use when order doesn't matter (e.g., choosing a committee).

Conditional Probability

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). In two-way tables, restrict your denominator to the given condition row/column.

Normal Distribution / Z-Scores

z = (x − μ) / σ. About 68% within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ.

Worked Example — Combinations

How many ways can 3 students be chosen from a group of 8?

C(8,3) = 8! / [3! × 5!] = (8 × 7 × 6) / (3 × 2 × 1) = 336 / 6 = 56

Worked Example — Conditional Probability

Of 100 students: 40 play sports, 30 play music, 15 do both. Given a student plays sports, what's the probability they also play music?

P(music | sports) = 15/40 = 3/8

Perm vs. Combo

Arrangement/order/ranking → permutation. Selection/committee/group → combination.

Topic 5

Advanced Geometry

The hardest geometry questions involve circle theorems, similar triangles, and 3D solids. These are rules most students never explicitly learned.

Inscribed Angle Theorem

An inscribed angle = half the central angle that subtends the same arc. Inscribed angles that share the same arc are equal.

Tangent Lines

A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the point of tangency. Tangent segments from an external point are equal in length.

Similar Triangles

AA, SAS, SSS similarity. Corresponding sides are proportional. Set up and solve the ratio to find missing lengths.

3D Volume & Surface Area

Cone: V = ⅓πr²h. Sphere: V = (4/3)πr³, SA = 4πr². Cylinder: V = πr²h. Cross-sections of 3D shapes appear on SAT.

Worked Example — Inscribed Angle

A central angle subtends an arc of 110°. What is the inscribed angle that subtends the same arc?

Inscribed angle = 110° ÷ 2 = 55°

Worked Example — Similar Triangles

Two similar triangles have sides 3, 4, 5 and 6, x, 10. Find x.

Scale factor = 6/3 = 2, so x = 4 × 2 = 8

Common Trap

The inscribed angle is half the central angle — students often flip this and double instead.

Topic 6

Advanced Trigonometry

Beyond SOHCAHTOA: law of sines, law of cosines, and the unit circle are tested on both ACT and SAT hard questions.

Law of Sines

a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C. Use when you have two angles and a side (AAS/ASA), or two sides and a non-included angle (SSA).

Law of Cosines

c² = a² + b² − 2ab·cos C. Use when you have SAS (two sides and included angle) or SSS (all three sides).

Unit Circle Key Values

sin 30°=½, cos 30°=√3/2 · sin 45°=√2/2, cos 45°=√2/2 · sin 60°=√3/2, cos 60°=½ · sin 90°=1, cos 90°=0

Trig Identities

sin²x + cos²x = 1 · tan x = sin x / cos x · sin(−x) = −sin x (odd) · cos(−x) = cos x (even)

Worked Example — Law of Sines

In triangle ABC, angle A = 30°, angle B = 45°, and side a = 8. Find side b.

b/sin 45° = 8/sin 30°
b = 8 × (sin 45°/sin 30°) = 8 × (√2/2 ÷ ½) = 8√2 ≈ 11.31

Worked Example — Law of Cosines

Triangle with sides a = 5, b = 7, C = 60°. Find c.

c² = 25 + 49 − 2(5)(7)(0.5) = 74 − 35 = 39
c = √39 ≈ 6.24

When to Use Which

No right angle + given an angle-side pair → Law of Sines. No right angle + SAS or SSS → Law of Cosines.
